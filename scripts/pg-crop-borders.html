Picture Guide — strip borders, standardise sizes

Photos taken out of the printed guide carry a border drawn into the image itself: a 3px line with a 1px antialias fringe outside it, and on the red ones a second fringe inside too, so black borders are 4px and red ones are 5px. The app draws its own frame and its own OPTIONAL flag, so that border lands as a second outline just inside the first. This finds the border on each photo, trims it off, and puts the trimmed photo back under the same filename — nothing in the database changes. It then puts every photo back to the square it was shot at — 563×563, the same for every sheet — so all the boxes on the board are identical and each photo fills its own to the edge, with nothing cropped and nothing padded.

The exports are stretched, and this un-stretches them. Ethan shoots these at 1:1 on a phone, but they arrive out of the guide document at about 563×596 — taller than wide. That was established rather than assumed: the same photograph came out of the document in two different shapes (591×565 with a border on it, 563×594 without), and measuring the phone in both shows the same scene at the same fractions of the canvas, so the document reshaped the picture instead of cropping it. Undoing that canvas shape makes the phone measure 2.0529 against a real iPhone 13 Pro's 146.7/71.5 = 2.0517 — 0.06% out, from two independent exports. So scaling each photo back to a square is not a distortion, it is the repair of one.

This is the only step here that resamples, and it only ever scales DOWN (563-567 wide and 593-599 tall, both to 563), so no detail is invented. Two earlier approaches are what it replaced: padding out to a shared shape, which leaves a band that reads as a border whatever colour it is; and cropping to a shared square, which fits but takes 13-14px off the phones on the taller sheet.

Edit NAMES below for a new batch. The square is SQUARE, and .pg-frame in styles.css is square to match. If a future batch arrives already 1:1, this becomes a no-op rather than a problem.

Scan first and read the border column. A batch whose borders were already removed before saving should report none on every row — that is the expected result, not a failure, and it means the crop is a pure centring. Any row claiming a border on a photo you know is clean is a reason to stop rather than to continue: this trims pixels off originals and puts them back under the same name.
