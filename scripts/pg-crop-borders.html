Picture Guide — strip borders, standardise sizes

Photos taken out of the printed guide carry a border drawn into the image itself: a 3px line with a 1px antialias fringe outside it, and on the red ones a second fringe inside too, so black borders are 4px and red ones are 5px. The app draws its own frame and its own OPTIONAL flag, so that border lands as a second outline just inside the first. This finds the border on each photo, trims it off, and puts the trimmed photo back under the same filename — nothing in the database changes. It then crops every photo to one standard square — 563×563, the same for every sheet in the guide — so all the boxes on the board are identical and each photo fills its own to the edge.

Cropped, never padded and never scaled. Padding was tried first and is what this replaced: filling a photo out to a shared shape leaves a band around it, and a band around a photograph reads as a border no matter what colour it is. Two attempts at making that band invisible both failed in the same way — sampled at the photo's edge it came out pink on the red-celled shots, sampled further in it came out lighter than the vignetted edge. So the picture fills the box instead.

The crop window is chosen per photo, around its subject, not taken from the middle. That is what makes cropping affordable here. A centred crop would clip something on nearly every photo, because the phone sits close to an edge in most of them; picking the window that contains the subject leaves 16 of the 24 untouched, and the worst case among the rest is 13px off a subject nearly 600px tall. The subject is found as the dark or saturated thing on a pale backdrop, so the acrylic stand, its shadow and the backdrop's own vignette do not count as things to protect.

Edit NAMES below for a new batch. The square is SQUARE, and .pg-frame in styles.css is square to match — those two go together. A photo whose shorter side is under SQUARE cannot be cropped to it and is refused rather than quietly padded.

Scan first and read the border column. A batch whose borders were already removed before saving should report none on every row — that is the expected result, not a failure, and it means the crop is a pure centring. Any row claiming a border on a photo you know is clean is a reason to stop rather than to continue: this trims pixels off originals and puts them back under the same name.
